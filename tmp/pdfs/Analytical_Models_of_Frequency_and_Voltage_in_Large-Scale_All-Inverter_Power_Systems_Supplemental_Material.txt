The voltage response of a droop GFM consists of several steps, namely the reactive power measurement filter,
the Q-V droop, the voltage and current controllers, and the LCL filter. In our paper, we suggest that the relationship
between the change in reactive power seen by a GFM and the subsequent change in terminal voltage magnitude
can be well approximated by the following first-order differential equation.

In Fig. S1, we see the change in reactive power in the device base, α∆Qerror, has a nearly perfectly linear
relationship with the settling value of the change in terminal voltage ∆|V |. This linear relationship holds for both
small and large changes in reactive power, as indicated by the values on the x-axis. The different colored markers
in Fig. 1 represent the magnitude of the active power load step accompanying the reactive power load step in each
simulation run. From this data, we can reasonably deduce that the change in the slope of the best fit line is very
small when a simultaneous active power disturbance is applied, even if that active power disturbance is large, thus
validating our assumption that the effect of active power disturbances on voltage magnitude is far less than the
effect of reactive power disturbances. In the simulations used to generate the plot in Fig. 1, a GFM capacity of 200
MV A was used, but the Kv parameter was found to stay nearly constant for different rated capacities.
From the simulation data in Fig. S1, we find that the linear best fit line has a slope of 0.2214. We can calculate
the exact value of Kv by dividing the slope of the line by the droop coefficient, which was 0.05 in these simulations.

We use a Kv value of 5 in our paper to provide a slightly conservative estimate of settling voltage and to
compensate for small inaccuracies introduced by simplifying assumptions, e.g. neglecting any P-V dynamics,
estimating ∆Q via the proposed methodology.
The precise value of this parameter for a specific inverter where either the actual hardware or its EMT model
(developed by the vendor) is available, can be obtained through hardware testing in the laboratory environment,
such as the testing presented in [S2], or parameter identification analysis in the simulation environment such as the
input-output sensitivity analysis we utilized in this paper. Either approach offers an intrinsic order reduction since
only the dominant modes that are excited during transients of a specific variable, which is voltage in this case, are
captured. An analytical description to rigorously derive Kv is provided in the following.
